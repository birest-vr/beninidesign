Practical advice

Where to spend and where to save

Worth the money

  • Mattress and base. You spend a third of your life there and the difference shows from night one.
  • Furniture hardware. Hinges, full-extension runners, gas struts: the first components to fail.
  • Kitchen worktop. It works every day under knives, heat and acids. It ages worst if chosen badly.
  • Lighting. It shifts how everything else is perceived, and changing it later means opening walls.
  • Seating used daily. Main sofa and dining chairs: the rest can wait.
  • The design itself. It costs a fraction of the furniture and prevents mistakes that cost far more than the fee.

Where you can save

  • Closed storage units. Behind a closed door, a well-built melamine carcass is indistinguishable from a far dearer one.
  • Accessories and decoration. Vases, art, cushions: they change often and don't need to last twenty years.
  • Children's rooms. They change function every five or six years; buy for adaptability, not longevity.
  • Rugs in circulation routes. They wear out anyway; better to replace than restore.
  • Guest and secondary bedrooms. Low use, low investment.
  • Spectacular finishes in secondary spaces. Nobody sees the marble in the utility cupboard.
The 10% rule

Always hold back 10% of the total budget as a reserve. In practice almost every project meets at least one surprise: a dimension that doesn't work, a discontinued item, a drain to move. Those with a reserve solve it; those without cut the last remaining item, which is usually the lighting.

Frequently asked

On pricing

Does the design fee pay for itself?

Often yes, but indirectly. A correct layout avoids bespoke units that weren't needed; a clear materials schedule avoids the mark-ups that appear when a supplier has to "interpret"; critically reading quotes almost always reduces the cost of the kitchen and the joinery. On a €30,000 furnishing budget the typical optimisation margin is 10–20% — between €3,000 and €6,000.

We don't promise it as a sales claim: it depends how optimised your starting plan already was. But it's why the design is worth doing before buying, not after.

Why does the same kitchen cost twice as much from one retailer to another?

Because "the same kitchen" almost never is. Carcass thickness, door type, hinge and runner brand, worktop, appliances and — above all — what is included in the price: delivery, installation, removal of the old kitchen, connections, plinth, upstand, under-cabinet lighting. Always ask for the written specification item by item: that's where the difference lives, not in the rendering.

Is a bespoke kitchen worth it?

A modular kitchen almost always wins where the space is regular: industrial economies of scale are hard to beat. Bespoke makes sense in three cases: irregular or very narrow spaces, unusual heights to use up to the ceiling, and requirements no catalogue covers. The typical bespoke premium is 25–40% for the same materials.

How much does fitting add to the prices I see?

Far more than people expect, and it's almost never included in supply-only price lists. Orders of magnitude: parquet laying €20–45/m², porcelain €25–50/m² (up to €70 for large formats), kitchen installation €400–900, wardrobe assembly €150–400, wallpaper hanging €15–30/m². When comparing two quotes, always check whether fitting is in or out.

Do you give discounts?

The sliding scale for multiple rooms is already in the price list. We don't negotiate case-by-case discounts: that would mean the starting price was inflated. What we do gladly is reduce the scope when a budget is tight — fewer renders, fewer rooms, no construction drawings — so you pay less for less work, not less for the same work done worse.
